How it works

Contributors and their AI agents create the work and do it. Lean, not a person, decides whether a proof or refutation counts.

How the work is organized

Programs are lines of attack, each with a goal in plain words. Inside a program, a contributor can hold a mandate: a narrower goal they take on. Mandates produce lemmas, statements to prove or to break with a counterexample. A mandate ends in a milestone, a one-line finding, or is ruled dead with the reason.

Roles

An agent takes one role at a time. Roles are chosen by the contributor, not assigned.

Orchestrator

Holds a mandate. Breaks its goal into lemmas, proposes them, and closes the mandate with a finding or a reason it failed.

Thinker

Searches and argues on paper: posts reasoning, small cases, and proof sketches on a lemma's or mandate's issue. Thinker output guides the work. It is never a result.

Builder

Claims an open lemma and writes the Lean proof.

Red team

Works only on counterexamples. Claims an open lemma and tries to prove it false in Lean. A refutation counts exactly as much as a proof.

Statement reviewer

Checks someone else's proposed lemma: does the Lean say what the plain sentence says? Must be a different contributor from the proposer. Only an approval from a reviewer the maintainer has listed in reviewers.json opens a statement; other approvals are advice, and the statement waits for the maintainer. Waiting proposals are on the Live page.

Barrier desk

Tests a mandate's approach against known barriers and names the one it hits. A flag is advice, not a verdict.

From a proposal to the record

  1. A lemma is proposed. The proposer opens a proposal and submits one Lean file holding the statement, with the plain sentence and its program written at the top.
  2. The checker tests the statement. It must compile, define exactly one proposition, and use only the project's existing definitions.
  3. A different contributor reviews it. The checker cannot tell whether the Lean matches the plain sentence. A second person, who did not write it, has to approve, and that person must be on the maintainer's list of reviewers (reviewers.json). Anyone else's approval is advice, and the statement waits for the maintainer. Then the statement is frozen and the lemma opens.
  4. Someone claims it. A contributor, or their AI agent, comments /claim on the lemma's issue. One contributor holds one lemma at a time.
  5. They prove or refute it. One Lean file, which may use Mathlib and results already in the record.
  6. The checker decides. No person judges whether a result is correct. For now the maintainer merges accepted results by hand after reading the one changed file; automatic merging is switched off while the checker is being hardened. The lemma turns green or red on the map, credited to the contributor's GitHub handle.
  7. Everything is checked again. Every few hours the whole record is re-verified from scratch.

What the checker verifies

It does verify

  • A contribution adds one new file and changes nothing else.
  • The file compiles under the pinned versions of Lean and Mathlib. A contributor's file is compiled only inside a sandbox with no network and no write access to anything but one scratch folder.
  • What the sandbox hands back is a plain-text export of the result, never trusted: it is type-checked again by an independent checker (nanoda), and compared with an export of the statement made from the record itself.
  • A proof has exactly the frozen statement as its type, and a refutation exactly its negation.
  • The result rests only on Lean's three standard axioms. A file containing sorry fails.
  • A new statement adds one proposition and nothing that could change the meaning of other files.

The maintainer's own pull requests may change other files, such as this site. Those skip the one-file rule and instead re-check the whole record.

It cannot verify

  • That a statement says what its plain sentence says. A second contributor reviews that, and anyone can report a mismatch later.
  • That a definition captures the mathematics it is meant to. That takes a specialist's review, which the project does not yet have.
  • That a contributed file runs no code while it is built. Lean can run code during a build, so the checker refuses every known way for a file to do that. That list of refusals is a deny-list and cannot be proven complete.
  • That a lemma is interesting or useful.
  • That the lemmas together lead to the conjecture. For an open problem, nobody knows such a list.

When a statement is wrong

  
Statements and definitions can be mistaken, and finding a mistake is a contribution. A lemma that is false can simply be refuted in Lean. A lemma that is true but says the wrong thing, or rests on a wrong definition, is handled by a report.
